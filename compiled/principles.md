# Principles

Distilled intellectual tenets that recur across Luka Piškorec's writing and practice. Each is a short, citable formulation; for the full context and original phrasing see [`writings.md`](writings.md).

## On code and education

### Code is the third literacy

Coding belongs alongside reading/writing and numeracy as a foundational literacy. The mental skill it teaches — algorithmic thinking — is general and durable, regardless of which language or framework is current.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"In search for a method".

### Data and instructions are the same thing

The first conceptual hurdle in learning to code is that numbers (and data structures more generally) play two distinct roles: they can represent information to be stored, or instructions on how to operate on that information. This is the von Neumann architecture, and grasping it is what separates students who learned syntax from students capable of algorithmic thinking.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Learning to code".

### Computational thinking has six characteristics

Decomposition, pattern recognition, data representation, generalization, abstraction, and algorithms. Together they let a designer reduce a problem to a generic, reusable solution.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), appendix.

## On computational and digital design

### Computational design ≠ digital design

These are orthogonal concepts. *Digital design* is using computers as drafting / representation tools. *Computational design* takes advantage of a medium's computational capabilities — generating, informing, or steering the design through algorithmic procedures. You can have one without the other: physical minimal-surface form-finding is computational without being digital; CAD drafting is digital without being computational.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), appendix.

### Architecture should design "organization", not buildings

If the profession is to survive, architects must shift focus from designing single spatial objects to designing organization in a more general sense — managing complexity in processes, systems, and digital structures. This is what generative architects already do.

> Source: *Generative Architecture Q&A* (2023).

### Generative architects design collections, not objects

A traditional architect designs one building. A generative architect designs an algorithm that produces an entire collection of variations. Architecture's old imperative was to optimise toward a single best design; generative practice instead pursues a well-rounded, diverse collection where every piece is valued in its own way.

> Source: *Generative Architecture Q&A* (2023).

### Wicked problems do not get easier with better tools

Just because the tool is more complex, the underlying architectural problem does not get easier to solve. Many problems in architecture are wicked — they evade easy solutions regardless of the medium. Code is still a superpower for traditional architects, but it is not a shortcut.

> Source: *Generative Architecture Q&A* (2023).

## On computation and Nature

### Rules rule the world

All natural processes are governed by a finite set of interconnected rules. The rules themselves can be simple; the consequences of their interactions are not. Properly formulated, these rules can be translated into code and used to derive computational states.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Observations and principles".

### Object- vs function-oriented worldview

Two equally valid programming-paradigm lenses for describing the world: either everything is an *object* with internal states (attributes) and behaviours (methods); or everything is a *function* or a *datum*, with no internal state, where functions act on data.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Observations and principles".

### Principle of opposites

Newton's Third Law in computational form: the world is composed of opposites between which there is tension. Tension destroys opposites by making them identical. Given enough time, all tension is released and the system reaches a state of equilibrium.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Observations and principles".

### Nature/culture dichotomy

Culture imitates Nature initially, then strives to supersede her. We cannot make assumptions about Nature's true goals or purposes — we can only observe Culture's response to her.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), appendix.

### State of equilibrium

In a closed system, matter, energy, and information are conserved. The world is a closed system. All natural phenomena can be described as either states of equilibrium or states striving to reach one. The number of such states is potentially infinite.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Observations and principles".

### Entropy and the cooling fan

The Second Law applies to computation as well as physics, but with a difference: a computer can maintain near-permanent order in its computational state by dumping entropy through a cooling fan into the outside world. To simulate the real world inside a computer, entropy has to be reintroduced manually — and so it can be controlled.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Observations and principles".

### Discrete time and space

Time and space are continuous in physics but discrete in computation, because computer hardware works only with discrete units. By choosing a sufficiently small interval, the discretisation becomes indistinguishable from the physical world. The granularity of these two discretisations sets the limits of any computational state and reveals its digital nature.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Observations and principles".

## On the practice of generative art

### Open code is a mission

Code for generative work should be kept open: unminified, unobfuscated, available on public repositories, stored on-chain or on decentralised protocols where appropriate, and documented through writing and recorded talks. Development of open generative tools, the merging of artistic expression with scholarship, and the promotion of responsible Web3 practices are missions every generative artist should embrace.

> Source: {protocell:labs} bio (2023–2024).

### The collection over the object

Letting go of the imperative to produce and justify one single design, and instead focusing on the collection — where every piece is valued on its own terms — is liberating. Open questions remain about *who* curates a collection: the artist, the algorithm, or the collector?

> Source: *Generative Architecture Q&A* (2023).

### The agency of the computer

Computers are not just digital stand-ins for rulers, compasses and pantographs. Their built-in von Neumann architecture and the formal languages we have developed to instruct them give them *agency* — they are programmable drawing agents in their own right, enabling the exploration of designs previously exclusive to Nature.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"In search for a method" / appendix.

### On being a generative artist

To be an artist often means standing apart, and to be an artist who codes can feel even more isolating. Generative art sits at the intersection of multiple fields and domains of knowledge. Adopting it as a form of expression — so often misunderstood — takes a certain bravery.

> Source: *Artist and Computer* (2024), §"Conclusion".
